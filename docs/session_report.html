Driver gaze and hand monitoring, simulated drive

A synthetic driver drives a 58 second scripted route in Unity. Three virtual cameras stream to a Python program that runs MediaPipe and OpenCV while the drive is running. Nothing here comes from a real person, a headset or a physical camera.

Questions

1. Can the live pipeline tell where the driver is looking, and when the gaze reaches a hazard?

In this simulation, yes. After a 12 second calibration on four known targets, the gaze yaw estimate was off by 1.17° on average (RMSE 2.82°, 867 frames) and the zone was right in 99.9 % of steady frames. The two reaction times were measured +114 ms and +196 ms from the true values (0.568 s against 0.454 s, and 1.307 s against 1.111 s). One video frame is about 51 ms.

The subject is clean and synthetic, and the thresholds and calibration were tuned on earlier runs of this same simulation, so these numbers are optimistic.

2. Does talking to a passenger slow the reaction to a hazard?

In the script it does. With eyes on the road the gaze reached the pedestrian 0.57 s after the pedestrian appeared, braking started after 0.68 s and the closest approach was 8.5 m. While talking and looking at the passenger it was 1.31 s, 1.37 s and 2.8 m, so the gaze reaction was 0.74 s slower. During the conversation the driver looked at the mirror or passenger 38 % of the time (baseline 11 %), with 2 glances longer than 2 s.

The two delays (0.45 s and 1.05 s) and the glance times were written into the script. This only shows the system can measure a difference of that size. It says nothing about real drivers.

3. Can blinks, speech and hand contact be tracked at the same time?

Blinks: 17 found for 17 rendered (recall 100 %, precision 100 %), 9 to 24 per minute across phases. Speech: precision 99 %, recall 97 % against the moments the mouth was moving, and it lags the start of a phrase by up to a second. Hands: both hands on the wheel in 91.5 % of frames. The right hand reached away from the wheel 2 times and 2 were found.

The hand camera is a front-diagonal view from the dashboard. MediaPipe hand landmarks were found in 99 % of frames. Wheel contact and reaches are decided with OpenCV skin segmentation in fixed image regions, and a reach is the right hand leaving the wheel region.

Everything above ran during the drive: 1123 frames at 19.4 fps, median 204 ms from capture to analysed frame (267 ms at the 95th percentile).

How it works

Unity plays the drive in real time. The driver model has scripted eye saccades, blinks, head turns, talking, steering, a two-handed grip and one hand reaching toward the centre display. Three virtual cameras (face, hands and wheel, outside view) are sent as JPEG over a local socket.

The Python side runs MediaPipe face landmarks (iris, blendshapes, head pose) on every frame. Gaze yaw is a linear fit of head yaw and iris position, calibrated on the first 12 s. Zones are classified by the nearest calibrated target with a two-frame debounce. Glances, eyes-off-road time, blinks, PERCLOS, speech and reaction time are computed as frames arrive. The simulator's own values are stored separately and used only for calibration and for the comparison panels.

With a real camera the receiver would read a webcam instead of the socket and the rest stays the same. A headset covers the eyes, so a cabin camera can only estimate gaze in a physical simulator or screen setup. With a Quest 3 the gaze would have to come from the headset itself, which it does not provide.

Limits

  • One synthetic subject and one session, with clean rendering: no lighting changes, glasses or occlusion.
  • Calibration uses labelled targets from the simulator. A real participant would need an on-screen target routine.
  • Hazard delays, glance times and speech are scripted.
  • Thresholds (blink 0.30, speech, skin fraction, zone debounce) were tuned on this simulation and not cross-validated.
  • The skin regions for wheel contact are fixed for this camera position and skin tone.
  • Only horizontal gaze is used. Vertical iris position was too noisy.
